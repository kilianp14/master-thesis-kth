This chapter attempts to synthesize the findings from the analysis to assess the broader implications of the results and the practicability of the proposed design. We first evaluate the effectiveness of risk control in providing formal behavioral bounds in Section \ref{sec:power_of_formal_guarantees}, followed by an analysis of the fundamental constraints that limit fast-path execution in Section \ref{sec:fast_path_viability}. Building on these observations, Section \ref{sec:practicality_of_conformal_consensus} assesses the practical viability of the conformal consensus approach compared to established architectures. Finally, we detail the theoretical and methodological limitations of the current prototype in Section \ref{sec:limitations} and outline essential directions for future work in Section \ref{sec:future_work}.


\section{The Power of Formal Guarantees}
\label{sec:power_of_formal_guarantees}
The empirical evaluation confirms that Rolling Risk Control can effectively regulate fast-path routing with sub-millisecond overhead. By dynamically tracking the target risk and error rates across varying geographic clusters, the online calibration routine demonstrates that statistical formal methods can reliably bound adaptive protocol behavior without becoming a computational bottleneck. Specifically, the analysis confirms that the target risk parameter can provide a simple, effective interface for managing the trade-off between optimistic execution and system stability. Because the conformal wrapper guarantees the error rate regardless of the underlying model's accuracy, developers are free to employ anything from simple, locally observable heuristics to complex machine learning models without compromising the formal boundaries.

While this thesis evaluates \gls{CRC} specifically in the context of consensus fast paths, the underlying methodology is applicable to a much broader class of distributed algorithms. Beyond consensus routing, this template for online risk calibration could be applied to several areas of distributed systems that rely on uncertain runtime decisions. Some consensus-relevant examples could include message batching, leader migration or complete cluster reconfiguration, quorum selection, or speculative execution on the application side.

Overall, these empirical results demonstrate that conformal methods, specifically Rolling Risk Control, can effectively regulate distributed routing decisions when wrapped around lightweight heuristics. This work offers a template for integrating formal guarantees into distributed protocols, showing that they are highly accessible, simple to integrate, and therefore practical for regulating distributed systems.


\section{Fast Path Viability}
\label{sec:fast_path_viability}

The theoretical latency reduction offered by the fast path was shown to be modest from the outset. In practice, the requirement for a larger fast quorum counteracts much of this benefit, particularly when the leader is strategically positioned relative to the rest of the cluster. This observation aligns with existing literature questioning the practical end-to-end performance gains of fast-path consensus over classic variants \cite{Junqueira-ClassicVsFastPaxos-2007}.

Because every proposal directly competes for the next sequential index in the log, contention is also an inherent property of the total-order abstraction. This structural bottleneck is the motivation behind partially ordered consensus designs, which exploit application-side command commutativity to avoid strict sequential conflict \cite{Moraru-Epaxos-2013}. The derived heuristic already highlights that the operational frame in which the fast path succeeds reliably is remarkably narrow for replicated logs. According to it, under one-way network latencies of approximately 20~ms, which is common for an intra-continental cluster, sustaining a 90\% fast-path success probability restricts the aggregate request rate across all other nodes to merely four requests per second.

Additionally, there exists a fundamental asymmetry in the cost-benefit trade-off: a successful fast-path proposal saves at most one message delay, whereas a collision triggers collision detection, slow-path recovery, and potential client retries. Wide-area deployments amplify this imbalance. While geo-replicated systems stand to gain the most from eliminating forwarding delays, their higher network latencies proportionally expand the time window during which concurrent proposals overlap. Consequently, the environments where optimistic fast paths are theoretically most desirable are precisely those where their underlying assumptions are weakest.

Ultimately, the optimism of fast-path execution for replicated logs is fragile, delivering negligible end-to-end latency gains even under ideal conditions, as shown in the analysis.


\section{Practicality of Conformal Consensus}
\label{sec:practicality_of_conformal_consensus}

Modern, fully fledged consensus protocols like OmniPaxos \cite{Ng-OmniPaxos-2023} offer a highly dependable, extensible, and failure-tolerant foundation that is strictly superior under sustained, heavy traffic. Furthermore, significant performance improvements can already be extracted from these classic architectures through system-level optimizations, such as flexible phase quorums \cite{Howard-FlexiblePaxos-2016}, caching \cite{Ng-UniCache-2023}, and strategic leader placement and general cluster setup optimization.

The analysis demonstrates, however, that slight end-to-end latency gains of a hybrid protocol materialize under highly idealized conditions. Practical viability strictly demands a highly stable cluster topology with minimal node failures, workloads characterized by periods of low contention where fast paths can succeed, and specific geographic configurations where fast-path routing holds a distinct physical advantage. Additionally, deployment can only be imagined when the goal is to decrease end-to-end latency and the across-node message passing is the primary bottleneck. This casts severe doubt as to the practicality of the hybrid protocol presented. If a complete hybrid protocol can be designed, there is still a high chance that it can generally match or outperform Fast Paxos throughout.

Whether employing the \gls{CRC}/Rolling Risk Control layer in the way it was designed aids practicality cannot be fully determined based on the analysis, but it at least provides a mathematically grounded, interpretable tuning knob with the fast-path error rate.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

First and foremost, the proposed hybrid consensus protocol is strictly exploratory and incomplete. It currently lacks formal safety and liveness proofs, and it remains fundamentally unverified whether a fully safe and lively specification of this design is theoretically achievable. Methodologically, the predictor evaluated whether a fast-path execution can succeed, rather than determining if it will be faster than the standard execution path. Under conditions of low contention, network latency variations may allow a fast path to succeed without delivering any net performance benefit over the standard consensus flow. Formal guarantees based on end-to-end latency likely provide more insight, although it remains unclear if this is implementable.

Furthermore, several implementation decisions and environmental constraints affected the depth of the analysis. The adaptive mechanism relied on a fixed learning rate of $0.005$, adopted from \cite{Angelopoulus-NeuralInformationProcessing-2023}. Because no dedicated hyperparameter study was conducted, the system relied on batch calibration to shift the threshold into an operational range from where a small learning rate was assumed to be sufficient. This leaves the true sensitivity of the learning rate unexplored. From a system perspective, the prototype operates entirely in memory and omits the disk I/O required for durable logging and recovery. While disk overhead is likely not the dominant latency factor in the distributed setups studied, the computational demands of the adaptive variant could introduce unmeasured storage bottlenecks. Finally, unpredictable network jitter in the public cloud environment introduced notable latency variance within and across test runs, which severely hindered progress in the evaluation.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future_work}

The primary theoretical priority is to fully formalize the hybrid consensus protocol. This involves developing rigorous safety and liveness proofs, specifying explicit requirements for safe leader election, and defining a formal log synchronization phase during leader transitions. From a methodological standpoint, future evaluations could investigate  transitioning from predicting fast-path success to providing concrete guarantees on total end-to-end latency. Ensuring that the fast path is triggered only when it yields a real latency reduction would eliminate the need for extensive prior network topology mapping. The success heuristic could still be a valuable part in achieving this.

From a practical infrastructure standpoint, future implementations should incorporate durable logging to stable storage. This will allow researchers to evaluate how the hybrid mechanism performs under realistic I/O constraints. Finally, integrating this adaptive risk-controlled framework into established consensus architectures, such as Nezha \cite{Geng-NezhaConsensus-2022}, represents a promising direction. Combining adaptive prediction with a protocol already optimized for fast-path execution could directly address the primary applicability bottlenecks observed in this study.
