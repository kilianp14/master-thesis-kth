Modern applications running on the Internet are increasingly distributed to enable scalability, improve service availability, and decrease user latency. In a distributed system, individual components can only coordinate via message passing over a network, making it naturally volatile and prone to partial failures of independent subsystems \cite{Coulouris-DistributedSystems-2005}. This failure model and the pessimistically asynchronous nature of communication networks, preventing a global notion of time, make many conceptually simple problems notoriously difficult. One example is commonly referred to as the \textit{consensus} problem \cite{Coulouris-DistributedSystems-2005, Fischer-DistributedConsensus-1985, Dwork-ConsensusPartialSynchrony-1988} and requires multiple processes to decide on the same value in a strongly consistent manner.

Research on this problem has come a long way since the \textit{Paxos} algorithm for solving consensus \cite{Lamport-Paxos-2001} was designed. \textit{Fast Paxos}, for instance, introduced an optimization eliminating a one-way delay in case of no concurrent proposals \cite{Lamport-FastPaxos-2006, Junqueira-ClassicVsFastPaxos-2007, Zhao-FastPaxos-2015}. In practice, these algorithms are commonly extended to maintain a continuously growing \textit{distributed log} instead of just being used to decide on a single value. Such a distributed log enables the implementation of \gls{SMR} for processing deterministic operations on multiple nodes, providing guaranteed convergence to the same state \cite{Schneider-ReplicatedStateMachines-1990}. This is an important abstraction for applications like distributed databases \cite{Corbett-GoogleSpanner-2013, Taft-CockroachDB-2020, etcd-Docs} and coordination services \cite{Burrows-Chubby-2006, Hunt-Zookeeper-2010}.

While consensus-based distributed logs inherently provide strong safety guarantees, their poor scalability and low throughput can dominate end-to-end system performance, in particular in geo-replicated deployments \cite{Stathakopoulou-RSMScalability-2022, Ryabinin-SwiftPaxos-2024}. As a result, a significant portion of current research focuses on reducing the overhead of log replication by using the optimistic fast paths originally devised for Fast Paxos. Fast-path algorithms, however, rely on favorable execution conditions, which rarely hold in production systems subject to churn, faults, skewed workloads, or even targeted attacks. In such collision-heavy scenarios, the expensive recovery mechanisms necessary to satisfy safety invariants degrade performance even below that of conservative protocols \cite{Junqueira-ClassicVsFastPaxos-2007}.

\section{Problem}
\label{sec:problem}
Much of the current research on \gls{SMR} departs from the classic notion of a single, totally ordered replicated log to a command ordering mechanism \cite{Ryabinin-SwiftPaxos-2024, Moraru-Epaxos-2013, Park-Curp-2019} to employ a fast path with minimal cost for mistakes. However, treating log-free or partially ordered consensus as a general replacement for replicated logs may fail to provide the specific guarantees required by all services. Instead, there might also exist possibilities to gate fast paths to avoid the majority of collisions in the first place while keeping a replicated log as the core abstraction layer.

This thesis aims to explore the usability of a hybrid consensus protocol in which the usage of \gls{CRC} \cite{Angelopoulus-ConformalRiskControl-2025} regulates when the system can take the fast path and when it must revert to the regular (more conservative) path. This approach can be used to provide distribution-free, finite sample guarantees on a chosen rate of fast path mistakes.
Given this goal, we can formulate the central research question as follows:

\begin{quote}
    \textit{Can fast-path regulation of a consensus protocol for a replicated log abstraction be formalized as conformal risk control in a way that improves latency while keeping mistakes bounded?}
\end{quote}
In addition to this main question, the work will tackle these supporting problems:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\roman*.]
    \item What architectural modifications are required to ensure safety and liveness in a consensus protocol where nodes independently and dynamically switch operating modes?
    \item Which locally observable runtime metrics can accurately predict fast-path success?
    \item What overheads does a regulator for conformal prediction introduce?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Contributions}
This thesis contributes the following:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item An exploration into the design of a hybrid consensus protocol in which followers can independently decide when to use the fast path.
    \item A formalization of fast path regulation as conformal risk control.
    \item A practical nonconformity score and online calibration method that can track a target rate of fast path mistakes under realistic conditions.
    \item An empirical study, which compares end-to-end latency in specific cluster setups in comparison to baselines.
    \item Open-source artifacts for the protocol and regulator libraries, and reproducible experiment scripts.
\end{enumerate}

The application of conformal risk control to the domain of distributed consensus is, to our knowledge, novel. The findings could be of interest to distributed systems researchers and backend engineers who deal with the volatility of optimistic protocols in geo-replicated environments.

\section{Research Methodology}
We outline the research methodology following the structure suggested by Håkansson \cite{Hakansson-ResearchMethods-2013}. Accordingly, this thesis uses a quantitative research methodology, a positivist research paradigm, and a deductive research approach. This is appropriate because the research aims to test a hypothesis about the effect of \gls{CRC}-based fast-path regulation on system performance and mistake rates using objectively measurable variables such as latency and fast-path mistake rate. The study assumes that system behavior can be observed, measured, and analyzed statistically, and that cause–effect relationships between protocol behavior and performance can be established through controlled experiments.

The primary research methods adopted in this thesis are experimental and empirical. The study is experimental because it involves implementing a regulator, manipulating system conditions, and measuring the resulting effects on performance and mistake rates under controlled conditions. It is empirical because the conclusions are based on observed data collected from simulations and experiments. Using statistical analysis, we will compare different deployments and see whether the proposed approach can result in improvements over the baselines. To ensure research quality, the work will address replicability through controlled experimental setups and publicly available artifacts that allow reproduction of results.

\section{Delimitations}
This thesis is highly exploratory, as there is little established methodology for designing and evaluating hybrid consensus approaches. Consequently, this work does not attempt to develop a fully fledged protocol with formal safety and liveness proofs. Instead, it focuses on observing system behavior under ideal conditions to determine whether a hybrid consensus approach is viable and has practical utility.

Regarding the system environment, this research assumes a fail-stop failure model without recovery from stable storage, thereby excluding the I/O overhead associated with durable logging. It does not address Byzantine faults, where malicious actors could intentionally circumvent the safety invariants of the consensus algorithms. Furthermore, the scope is restricted to software-level regulation at the consensus layer. It explicitly excludes hardware-based network optimizations or modifications to the underlying transport protocols.

Finally, this study is designed to isolate the overhead of the consensus mechanism. It does not account for highly complex application-level state machines, as large execution overheads at the application layer could shift the overall performance bottleneck away from the network and the consensus protocol itself.
